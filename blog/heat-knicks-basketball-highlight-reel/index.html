Heat vs. Knicks: How to Film Basketball Highlights and Social Clips

Tonight’s Miami–New York matchup is a timely reminder that a strong sports edit starts before the game. If you want a highlight reel and social cutdowns from one basketball shoot, plan your coverage for the finished formats—not just for the live action.

November 27, 2026 · Film Stop

Plan your sports video

One game can support more than one edit

The Miami Heat and New York Knicks are scheduled to meet at Madison Square Garden on Friday, November 27. A single game can lead to a recap, a longer highlight reel, player or coach interviews, and short vertical clips. Each version has a different pace and frame, so it helps to make those deliverables part of the shot plan. See the NBA game listing.

Film Stop is not affiliated with the NBA, the Heat, or the Knicks. These are general production tips for filming basketball with the proper venue permission.

1. Decide what the edit needs to do

Before the game, confirm who will watch each video and where it will appear. A quick social recap may need a strong opening and a few concise moments. A team profile may need warmups, interviews, details about the venue, and reactions. An extended highlight reel may need enough coverage to make the flow of play understandable. Make a simple deliverables list so the crew knows what to capture.

2. Give each camera a defined job

With multiple cameras, avoid having every operator follow the ball. A locked wide angle can preserve context, while a second camera follows the play and another gathers the bench, coaches, and crowd. Confirm each position with the organizer, keep walkways clear, and stay out of players’ and officials’ paths. If you are working solo, prioritize a reliable master angle and plan separate moments for reactions and details.

3. Capture the moments that make a sequence

A highlight is more satisfying when viewers can follow its setup and payoff. Look for the build-up, the key play, and the reaction afterward. Also gather establishing shots, warmups, the bench, the crowd, the scoreboard where permitted, and quieter transitions. These cutaways help editors bridge action and keep a short recap from feeling like a random collection of shots.

4. Frame with vertical versions in mind

If the client wants vertical social clips, discuss that before recording. Keep the key subject in a useful part of the frame, leave enough room for a vertical crop when shooting wide, and avoid cutting off the ball or player during fast movement. If possible and permitted, capture a dedicated vertical angle instead of expecting every horizontal shot to crop cleanly.

5. Record atmosphere and usable dialogue separately

Court squeaks, whistles, crowd response, and the energy around the bench help a recap feel present. Record clean room tone and atmosphere when you can, and use a separate microphone setup for interviews or commentary. Monitor with headphones, check levels, and follow venue restrictions. Keep cables and stands out of active paths.

6. Mark moments while they are fresh

Keep a simple timecode or play log for standout moments, interview names, and anything the client specifically wants included. A quick note can save time during review and editing, especially when the footage includes long stretches of play. Make sure timecode notes match the camera files and recording settings.

7. Back up, then edit for each platform

Protect the media as soon as practical: copy the files to separate storage and verify the copies before formatting cards. In the edit, build the main recap first, then create platform-specific cutdowns with clear pacing, readable captions, and safe text placement. Confirm music and footage rights, approved names and logos, and the client’s requested aspect ratios before delivery.

Plan the edit before tipoff

The Heat–Knicks game is today’s trending hook, but the production lesson applies to school, college, community, and professional basketball: decide on the final videos early, assign coverage, capture context and reactions, and protect the footage. That gives the editor more than a record of the score—it gives them material to shape into a story.
